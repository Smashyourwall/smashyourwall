What If She Says No When I Approach Her?

8 min read

What if she says no? I remember thinking that when it came to approaching a woman.

You see her, she's beautiful, you want to talk to her.

And then your brain immediately jumps ten steps ahead.

What if she rejects me? What if she laughs? What if she tells me to fuck off? What if I feel like an idiot?

And suddenly you're standing 20 feet away having an entire conversation with a woman who isn't even there.

And then the shield goes up trying to protect yourself from something that hasn't even happened yet.

Let's take a step back for a second.

How many times have you been told no in your life?

Think about when you were a kid.

"Dad, can I have another Pepsi?"

No.

"Can I have a cookie?"

No.

"Can I have a piece of cake?"

No.

You probably heard no hundreds or thousands of times before you were even old enough to understand what rejection meant.

Then you got older.

You asked for things.

You applied for jobs.

You asked someone for a raise.

You asked a girl out.

You wanted something and sometimes the answer was:

No.

And you survived every single one.

So why does the possibility of hearing it from an attractive woman suddenly feel like such a catastrophic event?

Because this one feels more personal. Of course it does. You're attracted to her. You want her to like you. The cake can’t like you back, you can only like the cake. So there is naturally more on the line. Your ego is involved!

But that doesn't mean the consequences are actually as enormous as your brain makes them feel.

Guess what pal? Whether you like it or not, You're going to hear no. A lot.

I've been told no more times than most people reading this have probably even approached.

I don't remember 99% of them.

I couldn't tell you their names.

I couldn't tell you what they were wearing.

I couldn't tell you exactly what they said.

Weird right?

But they happened.

I moved on.

And now they're gone.

That's what happens to most rejection!

Do you really remember the time your life changed because someone said no when you asked for cake?

You experience it, you process it for a few minutes, maybe you feel a little disappointed, and then life keeps moving.

You eat lunch (hopefully without cake).

You go to work.

You talk to your friends.

You wake up the next morning.

Nothing fucking happened.

But when you're inexperienced? You don't know that yet.

Your brain takes an unknown event and fills it with the worst possible outcome.

You don't know what rejection actually feels like, so you imagine it.

And imagination is very good at making things enormous.

You imagine the silence.

The awkwardness.

Her looking at you like you're some complete weirdo.

Everyone around you noticing.

You walking away humiliated.

You replaying it in your head for the next three years.

And maybe something mildly awkward does happen.

Maybe she says no.

Maybe she isn't interested.

Maybe the interaction is a little uncomfortable.

So what?

You just experienced the thing you were afraid of.

And you're still standing.

The strange thing about rejection is that you can spend years being terrified of an experience that lasts about ten seconds.

You walk over.

You say hello.

She isn't interested.

"Sorry, I'm not interested."

"Alright, no worries. Have a good day."

And then you walk away.

That's it.

The entire monster you spent 20 minutes imagining just turned into a less than ten-second interaction.

And once you've experienced that enough times, guess what happens?

You go talk to someone else.

This is why I think people misunderstand confidence.

They imagine a confident guy as someone who somehow knows he's going to succeed.

That's not the only definition of what confidence has to be.

Sometimes confidence is simply knowing:

"Even if this goes badly, I'll be fine."

You're not confident because you know she'll say yes.

You're confident because you've learned that you'll survive if she says no.

And you don't learn that by thinking about it harder.

You learn it by experiencing it.

Again.

And again.

And again.

Eventually, rejection starts becoming boring.

And honestly?

Boring is exactly where you want it.

The first few times might stick with you.

The hundredth time probably won't.

At some point, "no" becomes just another possible answer.

Not a catastrophe, just some information.

She's not interested.

Okay.

Next.

And that's when rejection stops controlling your behavior. That moment is one of the most freeing moments I have ever been lucky enough to live through.

I'm not telling you that you should become some emotionless machine who doesn't care what women think.

If you're attracted to someone and she rejects you, you're probably going to feel something.

That's human.

The goal isn't to stop feeling.

The goal is to stop letting the possibility of feeling bad control what you do.

Ding ding ding, that's the difference!

Because think about what happens when you let the possibility of rejection make your decisions for you and everyone else on the planet.

You see her.

You want to talk to her.

You don't.

She walks away.

And you tell yourself:

"I'll do it next time."

But you don't know if there will be a next time.

Maybe you never see her again.

And now you've avoided ten seconds of possible discomfort in exchange for something much worse:

wondering what would have happened.

OOF.

I've experienced both.

And I'd much rather hear "no" than spend time wondering.

A rejection gives you an answer.

Doing nothing gives you a question.

So what if she says no?

Who cares?

You might feel embarrassed for thirty seconds.

Maybe you'll feel disappointed.

Maybe you'll think about it for the rest of the afternoon.

And then you'll eventually move on.

You'll probably even forget about her.

You'll probably forget that you were ever afraid of approaching her in the first place.

Because that's what happens when you accumulate enough experience.

The things that once felt enormous become ordinary.

The woman you were terrified to approach becomes one conversation.

The rejection you thought would destroy you becomes one sentence.

And the fear that kept you standing 20 feet away becomes something you barely recognize.

Not because you learned how to guarantee a yes.

Because you learned that you can handle a no.

So the next time you're standing there thinking:

"What if she says no?"

Walk over anyway.

Say hello.

Find out.

You know you can’t guarantee a yes.

What you can guarantee is giving yourself the chance to hear yes.

And that's a trade I'm willing to make.
