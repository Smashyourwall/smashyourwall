What If She Says No When I Approach Her?

8 min read

What if she says no? Every guy thinks this before he takes a step.

You see her, she's attractive, you want to introduce yourself—and your brain immediately jumps ten steps ahead.

What if she rejects me? What if she laughs? What if she tells me to get lost? What if I look like an idiot?

Suddenly you're standing 20 feet away, having an entire imaginary conversation with someone who doesn't even know you're there.

And then the shield goes up to protect yourself from something that hasn't even happened.

Let's take a step back for a second.

How many times have you been told no in your life?

Think about when you were a kid.

"Dad, can I have another soda?"

No.

"Can I have a cookie?"

No.

"Can I stay up late?"

No.

You heard no thousands of times before you were even old enough to understand what rejection meant.

Then you got older. You applied for jobs, asked for raises, or pitched ideas. You wanted something, and sometimes the answer was:

No.

And you survived every single one.

So why does hearing it from an attractive woman suddenly feel like a catastrophic event?

Because this one feels personal. You're attracted to her, and you want her to respond positively. The cake can't reject you—you just eat it or you don't. But when you talk to her, your ego is suddenly in the arena.

That doesn't mean the consequences are anywhere near as enormous as your brain makes them feel.

Whether you like it or not, you're going to hear no. A lot.

I've been told no more times than most people reading this have ever approached.

I don't remember 99% of them.

I couldn't tell you their names, what they were wearing, or exactly what they said.

They happened, I moved on, and now they're gone.

That's the reality of most rejection.

Do you remember a single day your life was ruined because you were told no as a kid? You felt disappointed for three minutes, and then life kept moving.

You eat lunch, go to work, talk to friends, and wake up the next morning.

Nothing catastrophic happened.

When you're inexperienced, your brain fills an unknown event with the worst possible scenario.

Since you haven't built the evidence that you'll be fine, imagination makes the situation feel enormous.

You imagine awkward silence, her looking at you like you're a creep, everyone around watching, and you walking away humiliated for three years.

Even if an interaction is slightly uncomfortable or she isn't interested...

So what?

You just experienced the thing you were afraid of—and you're still standing.

You can spend years being terrified of an interaction that lasts ten seconds.

You walk over, say hello, she says she's not interested, you say "No worries, have a great day," and you walk away.

That's it.

The entire monster in your head turned into a ten-second interaction. Once you experience that enough times, you simply move on with your day.

This is why people misunderstand confidence.

Confidence isn't knowing you'll always succeed.

Real confidence isn't believing she'll definitely say yes. It's knowing you'll be completely fine if she says no.

You don't learn that by overthinking. You learn it through experience—again and again.

Eventually, rejection becomes boring. And boring is exactly where you want it.

The first few times stick with you; the hundredth time doesn't. "No" becomes just another data point. Not a catastrophe—just information.

She's not available or interested? Cool. Next.

That is when fear stops dictating your decisions.

The goal isn't to become an emotionless robot. Feeling a brief sting of disappointment when you like someone is human.

The goal is to stop letting the fear of discomfort dictate your actions.

Think about what happens when you let fear make decisions for you.

You see her, you want to talk to her, you don't, and she walks away. You tell yourself, "I'll do it next time." But you might never see her again.

You traded ten seconds of minor discomfort for weeks of wondering what would have happened.

A rejection gives you an answer. Inaction leaves you with regret.

So what if she says no?

You might feel embarrassed for thirty seconds, and then you move on. The things that once felt overwhelming become ordinary through experience.

You can never guarantee a yes. But by staying on the sidelines, you guarantee a zero. Walk over, say hello, and give yourself the chance to find out.
